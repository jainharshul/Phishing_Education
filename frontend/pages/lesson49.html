Incident Management Workflow

Detection is only the first step in defense. Once a phishing attempt is identified—whether automatically or through a user report—the organization must transition from analysis to incident management. This workflow orchestrates containment, eradication, and recovery to minimize impact and prevent recurrence.

1. Detection and Validation. When an alert triggers, SOC analysts verify authenticity by examining headers, routing paths, and payload hashes. Automated correlation with threat-intelligence feeds confirms whether indicators (domains, IPs, attachments) appear in known campaigns.

2. Containment. The affected email is quarantined across all mailboxes using API recall or server-side deletion. Any recipients who interacted—clicked links, opened attachments—are identified. Endpoint detection tools check for signs of compromise, while identity systems revoke active sessions associated with exposed credentials.

3. Eradication. Malicious artifacts are isolated and analyzed in sandboxes to derive indicators of compromise (IOCs). These IOCs are propagated to firewall, proxy, and endpoint control lists to block recurrence. Compromised credentials are reset, and system logs are reviewed for lateral movement.

4. Communication. The incident response team notifies relevant stakeholders—IT, management, legal, and affected users. Clear, factual messaging prevents rumor and panic. If customer data is involved, compliance officers assess breach notification requirements under relevant regulations.

5. Post-Incident Analysis. Forensics reconstructs the full timeline—delivery vector, user interaction, detection latency, and response time. Lessons learned are documented in a root-cause analysis. Where possible, data from the incident—sanitized of personal details—is recycled into model retraining to prevent similar misses.

6. Continuous Improvement. Metrics such as mean time to detect (MTTD), mean time to respond (MTTR), and residual risk are tracked over time. Playbooks are updated, automation scripts refined, and awareness programs adjusted based on observed behavioral patterns.

Incident management is both a technical and procedural discipline. It requires synchronization across SOC analysts, system administrators, HR, and leadership. The ultimate goal is not merely recovery but resilience—emerging from each incident smarter, faster, and less vulnerable. In a mature cybersecurity posture, every phishing email that slips through is not a failure, but a catalyst for systemic strengthening.
