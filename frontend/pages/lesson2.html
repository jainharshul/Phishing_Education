History of Email Phishing

Email phishing is the natural evolution of historical scams adjusted to modern technology. It is important to understand the history of phishing as that gives insights into the origins of its methods and historical defences which stand the test of time.

The first major wave of phishing began in the mid-1990s on America Online (AOL). Attackers, often called "phreaks," used instant messages and emails that appeared to be from AOL administrators. These messages would ask users to "verify" their account or confirm their billing information. If the user fell for it and entered their password, the attacker would steal their AOL account. The stolen accounts were then used to send more phishing messages or for other scams. This was the proving ground for digital deception.

As the internet grew, so did phishing. The late 1990s and early 2000s became the era of the "Nigerian Prince" scam, also known as a 419 scam. This famous email promised the victim a huge share of a fortune in exchange for a small upfront fee to help transfer the money out of a foreign country. While poorly written and obvious to many, these scams were sent to millions of email addresses. They worked because they only needed a tiny fraction of people to believe the story and send money, making the attackers a profit. This was the start of mass, bulk-email phishing.

The mid-2000s saw phishing become a major criminal industry. As people began banking and shopping online, attackers started targeting financial institutions like PayPal and eBay. They would send mass emails warning of an "account problem" with a link to a fake website that looked almost identical to the real one. When users logged in, their credentials were stolen. This period showed a shift from simple greed to targeted financial theft on a massive scale, exploiting the growing trust people had in online services.

Today, phishing has split into two main paths. The first is still the mass spam email, sent to millions, hoping to catch anyone who isn't paying attention. The second, and far more dangerous, is highly sophisticated and targeted attacks. This includes "spear phishing," where attackers research a specific individual to create a highly personalized and convincing email, and "whaling," which targets high-level executives. Modern phishing emails can be nearly flawless, using real company logos, professional language, and information gathered from social media to build trust.

From the simple tricks on AOL to the targeted campaigns of today, the history of phishing is a story of criminals constantly adapting their lies to new technology and human psychology. By knowing this history, we can better understand that this is a persistent threat that requires our constant attention.
